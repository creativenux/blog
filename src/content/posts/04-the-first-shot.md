title: "The First Shot"
date: 2026-05-04

In 2022, I was running a small software company. We were good at building things. What we weren't good at was getting the next client.

I didn't notice how dangerous that was at the time.

I had spent the years before that working closely with founders, business owners and people with ideas. Most of them weren't technical and I kept seeing the same thing happen. They would make a decision about the product, change it a week later, change it back the week after. They would contract the build to freelancers who shipped just enough code to make the feature work. No documentation. No structure. Nothing that would survive the next person who had to touch it. Six months later, the system was unmaintainable and the founder didn't know why.

I thought I could fix that. I thought a company built around a technical partner, someone who could sit with founders and turn their ideas into systems that actually held up, would solve a real problem.

At the time, I was also working as a full-time backend engineer. I liked the work but not everything around it. It started to feel like I was solving interesting problems inside a space that kept getting smaller and that the parts of the job I disliked, the office requirement, the HR layer, were taking up more room than the work itself. I wanted something with more space to think and build.

So at midnight on my birthday, just after the COVID-19 lockdown, I decided to start a company. A year and a few months later, I registered Intrapair.

I unofficially started in 2021, running it on the side and using that year to learn, research and understand how business, team structure and strategy work. Basically, reading books.

The company fully started in 2022. Within a couple of months, there were five of us. Then ten. At its peak, twenty. Across the full life of the company, fifty-seven people worked with us at one point or another.

On paper, I was running the company. In practice, I was running the engineering team. 

I led the backend team, reviewed code, made system architectural designs, helped people debug and shipped features. I was where I was most comfortable.

That should’ve been a sign. It wasn’t.

There is a clean version of how it failed that I could tell. Market conditions, late client payments, the economy. All of it is true. None of it was the real reason.

The real reason is something I only really understood later: I built a company that was optimized for the version of me that already existed.

I had started Intrapair to be the technical partner that founders never have. I executed on that idea well. I sat with the founders. I turned ideas into production systems. I wrote documentation. I built things that survived the next person who had to touch them. The problem is that the company didn't only need a technical partner. It needed a founder. And I was so good at being the technical partner that I forgot to be the founder.

A service company lives or dies on a pipeline of new clients. A pipeline is built by consistently talking to prospects, not reviewing pull requests. I spent two years getting very good at the work and almost no time getting good at the business of selling the work. I had set out to fix a problem I saw in other founders, founders who focused on what they knew and let the rest drift and I made a smaller version of the same mistake myself.

I said part of this plainly in an email to my team in August 2024, when we transitioned everyone off full-time employment:

> I lost sight of what’s important. I was too focused on the engineering department, almost entirely neglecting the marketing and sales department, which is our primary source of new projects.

That was true. The email named the symptom. The cause was that I had built a role for myself inside the company that let me stay an engineer.

It didn't help that I was running other things in parallel. I wanted to pivot the company from services to products. We had a flagship product called FlipCBT, a continuation of the computer-based test software I had built for secondary schools, rebuilt as something bigger. 

Alongside that I was running another startup called Stitchvine, an app for fashion designers to manage their business effectively. Stitchvine never got traction and eventually shut down, but for a long stretch it was taking real time, attention and capital.

I told myself it was the same kind of work. It wasn't. Building your own product and running a service company are two different jobs because they optimize for different things. A service company optimizes for the next invoice. A product company optimizes for the next user. The day-to-day decisions are different. Trying to do both meant doing neither well at that stage.

Looking back, I think I was reaching for the founder side of the job through products because I hadn't found a way to reach for it through Intrapair itself. Building a new product felt like founder work. Reaching out to prospects did not. So I kept doing the thing that felt like founder work and avoided the thing that actually was.

By early 2024, payments from clients were getting delayed. We were funding payroll from personal accounts. I kept thinking it would correct itself. I think this is one of the mistakes founders make most often. They assume the situation will fix itself if they hold on a little longer. It will not, if the processes that produced the situation stay the same.

The harder thing to admit is that I noticed. By Q3 2023, it was clear the pipeline wasn't strong enough. Fixing that would have meant spending less time on engineering and more time trying to get customers. I didn't do that. I kept reviewing code. I kept shipping features. I told myself the next big project would land and reset the runway. In the same email to the team, I called this: *the management's failure to take proactive steps earlier. We knew something was broken but hoped it would self-correct.*

The hardest part wasn't shutting down full-time roles. It was sending a personalized email to each person. The same backbone, but each one had a specific thing in it. A moment I remembered. The number of months or years they had spent with us. Something I wanted them to know I had noticed.

Would I do it again? Yes. But not the same way. I'd make sure that getting customers is a daily activity, not something I got to after the real work was done. I'd pick one direction, services or product and stick with it long enough to see it work or fail. I'd act on bad signals when they were still small. Most of all, I'd stop confusing being good at the work with being good at the business of selling the work.

The first shot missed. That is what first shots do. The point of the first shot isn't to hit. It is to teach you how to aim.

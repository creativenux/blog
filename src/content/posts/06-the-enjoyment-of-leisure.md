---
title: "The Enjoyment of Leisure"
date: 2026-08-02
description: "A reflection on curiosity, learning, and the unexpected connections found in ordinary evenings"
topics: ["Awareness"]
---

Over the years, reading has quietly become my favourite way to spend free time.

The internet expanded that habit.

Short deep dive videos on YouTube replaced some of the books. Long conversation podcasts replaced the short videos. There was usually a conversation playing somewhere in the background while I made dinner.

Earlier this year, I started attending public lectures.

Not because I wanted another way to learn but because it just felt like an interesting way to spend an evening.

The first lecture almost didn’t happen.

It had been raining all day. The kind of evening where staying in my room feels like a sensible decision. I remember looking outside and wondering if leaving the house for a lecture on the Earth’s subsurface was really worth it.

Eventually, curiosity won.

By the time I arrived, the lecture had already started. I entered the room as quietly as I could.

My first thought was maybe I had come to the wrong place.

Almost everyone was dressed in suits or corporate clothes. I was easily the youngest person there.

The professor had spent more than twenty years trying to understand what lies beneath the Earth’s surface, studying geological structures and the data they leave behind to better understand the processes connected to climate change.

I don't remember every detail from that evening. 

What stayed with me was carbon capture and storage. 

Until then, I had never considered that carbon dioxide could be stored deep beneath the Earth's surface.

A couple of weeks later, I attended a lecture on faith, values and pluralism. 

Before that evening, I’d never heard the word pluralism. Even if someone had asked me to guess its meaning, I don't think I would have come close. 

What surprised me wasn't learning a new word. It was realizing the word described something I've always been surrounded by.

Another evening I listened to a lecture on how places are shaped by care and by the lack of it.

The researcher kept returning to rivers. If we take care of the river, eventually the river will take care of us. Clean water, healthier ecosystems, places people want to live. Reciprocity, he said, may take time.

Each lecture introduced me to ideas I probably would never have searched for on my own.

But after a while, I realized it wasn’t only the subjects bringing me back.

It was the people.

Most evenings, I was the youngest person in the room.

I liked that.

Everyone seemed to have arrived from a different direction.

Some asked questions that sounded like they had spent years thinking about the topic.

Others, like me, simply listened.

Then came a lecture on living with functional seizures.

At the entrance was a section for refreshments before everyone made their way into the cinema room.

An elderly gentleman standing in front of me as I walked in offered me a glass of milk and we started talking.

When I mentioned I was from Nigeria, he smiled and told me he had worked there as an engineer for seven years on Bonga FPSO.

He took my phone and showed me pictures of the ship on Google Images.

Before we walked into the cinema room, he said something almost in passing.

> Find what you love and make sure you do it wholeheartedly.

Then he introduced me to his wife as we walked in.

During the question and answer that followed after the researchers presented their findings, people slowly revealed why they had come.

Some had professional reasons.

Others had personal ones.

I realized I'd spent the whole evening assuming everyone was there for the lecture.

By the end, it seemed the lecture was simply the place that brought everyone together.

Around that time, I was exploring an idea for supporting people living with long-term health conditions, where loneliness and isolation become part of everyday life.

I didn’t expect to find myself in a room with so many different lives connected to the same problem.

The researchers.

The clinicians.

The families.

The people living through it.

They all arrived through different doors.

For a long time, I thought the enjoyment of leisure came from choosing what I wanted to consume.

A good book.

A movie.

A podcast.

An online lecture.

I still enjoy all of those.

What I didn't expect was that I'd remember the conversations more than the lectures. 

Much of what I learned from the lectures could have been watched later on a screen. 

The glass of milk could not.
